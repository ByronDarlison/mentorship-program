## Home

**Status: Agreed**

### One-to-one mentorship for meaningful business progress.

Running a business can feel lonely, even when you have good people around you. There are times when you are left working through an important decision on your own. Having someone you can speak openly with, who listens and brings a fresh perspective, can help you see what might be right in front of you.

The Example Chapter Mentorship Program connects members with experienced mentors for a year of learning and practical support. Through thoughtful questions and by sharing what they have learned, mentors help members find their own answers and make progress on what matters most to them.

For mentors, this is an opportunity to give back and keep learning. Exploring another entrepreneur’s challenges can bring fresh perspectives, test your assumptions and sharpen your thinking. Listening, asking thoughtful questions and sharing your experience can deepen your understanding of what you’ve learned and how you lead. It is also a chance to build a meaningful connection with another entrepreneur.

## The result we want

**Status: Agreed**

The program goal is simple:

> The Example Chapter Mentorship Program helps members make meaningful progress in their businesses through trusted mentoring relationships.

The program succeeds when:

- Each active pair receives check-ins at months three, six, nine, and twelve, or a final check-in when its relationship ends early.
- At least 80% of mentees included in final reporting describe meaningful or significant business progress through the mentorship.
- At least 80% of mentees included in final reporting describe meaningful or significant value from participating.
- At least 80% of mentors included in final reporting describe meaningful or significant value from participating.
- At least 80% of mentors included in final reporting are interested in mentoring again.

Final reporting includes relationships that end early and unanswered final requests after twenty-one days. No response counts as failure until corrected. The measurement section explains the calculation.

These measures support the relationship. They do not replace it.

A mentee's mentorship is unsuccessful if it does not help them make meaningful business progress. Enjoying the conversations does not change that result.

## How the program works

**Status: Agreed**

### 1. Apply

Mentees and mentors complete short applications.

After launch, applications are welcome year-round. Participants begin together in groups, called cohorts.
There may be a wait while we arrange suitable matches and prepare the next group.
The Chair decides when a cohort is ready. The first group may start with fewer than ten pairs.
Applying does not guarantee a place or a start date.

The program collects only the information needed for eligibility, matching, safety, and basic administration.

### 2. Match

AI compares the structured mentee and mentor information. It recommends possible matches and explains its reasoning.

The Mentorship Chair reviews every recommendation and approves every final match.

AI supports the decision. It does not make the decision.

### 3. Train together

Each cohort completes one joint, live, one-hour online training session.

Mentors and mentees attend together. A pair may begin after both people have completed the training, including a live make-up when the Mentorship Chair decides one is appropriate.

Each pair books its first 3–6 meetings before leaving training and shares the first meeting date with the Chair.

### 4. Hold the first meeting

The first mentoring meeting begins each pair's individual twelve-month cycle.

The day after the booked meeting, the mentee receives a brief email to confirm whether it happened or was rescheduled.
The cycle starts on the confirmed actual meeting date, not the training or booking date.

The first meeting is also the pair's introductory expectations conversation. No separate introductory call is required.

### 5. Meet monthly

Each pair meets for one hour in ten of the twelve months of its cycle.

At each meeting, top up the calendar to keep the next 3–6 meetings booked, or the remaining meetings near the end of the program. The mentee owns scheduling.

### 6. Check in quarterly

The pair completes simple check-ins at months three, six, nine, and twelve.

Each check-in asks how often the pair met, whether each participant is receiving meaningful value, and whether either person wants the Mentorship Chair to contact them.

### 7. Complete the cycle

The relationship completes twelve months after the first mentoring meeting.

At month twelve, the Chair asks the mentee about business progress and asks the mentor, “How would you feel about mentoring again?” Both share what value they received.

If the relationship ends early, these final questions are sent then and the later quarterly check-ins stop. The feedback still counts in program results.

If there is interest, Example Chapter may hold an optional annual gathering for current and past participants.

## The mentee's role

**Status: Agreed**

The mentee owns the relationship, the decisions, and the action.

The mentee is responsible for:

- Scheduling the meetings.
- Preparing the agenda.
- Naming what would be useful to discuss.
- Speaking candidly about the business and current reality.
- Making decisions.
- Taking action between meetings.
- Following through on commitments.
- Completing the short quarterly check-ins.
- Contacting the Mentorship Chair when something is not working.

The mentor helps the mentee find their own answers. The mentor may also share experience or a direct view when the mentee wants it. The mentee decides what to do.

## The mentor's role

**Status: Agreed**

The mentor brings experience, attention, questions, perspective, challenge, and encouragement.

The mentor is responsible for:

- Listening before offering a view.
- Asking one clear question at a time.
- Staying curious beyond the first answer.
- Asking what kind of help the mentee wants.
- Sharing relevant experience or a direct view when useful and requested.
- Challenging assumptions and inconsistencies respectfully.
- Returning decisions and responsibility to the mentee.
- Protecting confidentiality.
- Participating in quarterly check-ins.
- Contacting the Mentorship Chair when something is not working.

The mentor does not become the mentee's consultant, manager, or operator.

## Matching

**Status: Agreed**

Matching should connect the mentee's challenge or opportunity with the mentor's relevant experience and skills.

The program selects mentees first, then looks for mentors who fit their actual needs. A mentee is not promised a place until the Chair has approved a suitable match.

Mentors may be invited directly or express interest through the public website. A mentor who is not already known to the Chair has a short conversation with the Chair before approval. Every mentee also has a short readiness conversation with the Chair before selection.

During the readiness and matching review, the Chair confirms that a proposed pair can schedule remote meetings and checks for conflicts before approving the match.

AI will compare the structured application information and recommend potential matches with reasons.

The Mentorship Chair approves every final match.

The introduction gives both people a short, positive explanation of why the Chair believes the match could be useful. It does not show AI analysis, rankings, or alternatives.

If either participant has a concern after the relationship begins, they contact the Mentorship Chair.

The Chair handles each concern according to the situation. The program does not require a formal off-ramp process.

### Information used for matching

AI compares only the information needed to assess a useful relationship:

- The mentee's business, challenge or opportunity, and the experience they would value.
- The mentor's direct experience with comparable problems, functions, stages, and industries.
- Any practical scheduling limits confirmed during the readiness conversations.
- Known conflicts, competitive relationships, or exclusions checked before approval.

Industry experience can strengthen a match. It is not required when other experience is more relevant.

### AI recommendation format

AI recommends the strongest available mentor for each mentee. It may identify alternatives privately for the Chair when the evidence is close.

Each recommendation includes:

- The strongest reasons for the match.
- Any important gaps or risks.
- The application information that supports each reason.
- Any question the Mentorship Chair should resolve.

AI must not infer sensitive facts or invent missing experience. It must identify missing information instead.

The recommendation stays private. Participants do not receive rankings or alternative candidates.

### Chair decision

The Mentorship Chair reviews applications, recommendations, conflicts, and available capacity.

The Chair may accept a recommendation, select another mentor, or decide that no suitable match exists.

The Chair records the approved match and a brief reason. The Chair does not record confidential analysis in GitHub.

Only the approved matching tool may receive the coded matching information defined in the privacy section.

Mentors and mentees attend a live, one-hour online training session together before their mentoring meetings begin. We will work through the curriculum below, discuss examples, and practise a mentoring conversation. You will leave with shared expectations and your first 3–6 meetings booked.

### 1. What we are working toward

The program helps Example Chapter members make meaningful progress in their businesses through trusted mentoring relationships.

A mentor brings attention, questions and experience. A mentee brings what matters to them, a willingness to consider another perspective, and responsibility for deciding and acting. Together, they create space to think through something the mentee may have been wrestling with alone.

A useful conversation helps the mentee see more clearly and decide what to do next. Over time, it should also strengthen their ability to work through future challenges for themselves. Mentors learn too, as another entrepreneur's situation tests their assumptions and brings fresh perspectives.

### 2. Our commitments

#### Together

- Attend the joint one-hour training and book your first 3–6 meetings before leaving.
- At each meeting, keep the next 3–6 meetings booked, or the remaining meetings near the end of the program.
- Meet for one hour in 10 of the 12 months. Your twelve-month cycle begins when your first mentoring meeting takes place.
- Keep mentoring conversations confidential. Give each other your full attention and room to speak openly.
- Complete the short quarterly check-ins about meeting frequency and the value of participating. At the end, mentees also reflect on business progress and mentors on whether they would mentor again. Keep confidential conversation details out of these responses.
- Do not engage in paid services between each other.

#### The mentee's part

- Schedule the meetings.
- Come fully prepared to talk about what matters most.
- Listen, consider what the mentor shares, and decide what to do next.
- Complete what you commit to between meetings.

#### The mentor's part

- Listen and ask questions.
- Check in on progress toward prior commitments.
- Share relevant experiences.
- Point out possible blind spots.

### 3. A useful mentoring conversation

Focus, Explore, Help and Close are four useful moves. Use them naturally as the conversation needs them. There is no script to complete, and you do not need to solve everything in one meeting.

#### Focus: start with what matters to the mentee

> What would be most useful to discuss today?

When there are earlier commitments, check in on them. Ask one question, listen, then follow up on what you hear.

> What happened with the action you chose last time?

> What did you learn?

The mentee chooses what needs attention now. They may change their focus as their business or understanding changes.

#### Explore: listen before trying to help

Give the mentee time to finish. Notice their words and allow silence so they can think. WAIT, or “Why Am I Talking?”, is a useful private reminder for the mentor.

Ask one short, neutral question at a time. Follow the answer rather than preparing your next question while they speak. Reflect what you heard and check that you understood it.

Useful questions include:

- What else?
- What is the real challenge for you?
- What part of this is yours?
- What do you want to happen?

The first answer may only be the surface. Stay curious, but stop probing when the mentee is clear. “What” and “how” questions often make it easier to explore without feeling the need to defend a decision.

“Have you tried hiring a manager?” contains a suggested solution. “What is keeping this work with you?” leaves room for the mentee to discover what is happening.

Sometimes a different angle helps. Choose a prompt that fits what the mentee is working through:

- **Picture what better would look like:** “Imagine you came in on Monday and this was working the way you wanted. What would you notice first?”
- **Borrow another perspective:** “Who do you know who would handle this well?” Listen before following up with, “What might they do?”
- **Recall when things went better:** “When has this gone a little better?” Listen before asking, “What was different then?”

Ask one question at a time and follow the answer. These are options, not a script to complete. If imagining a complete resolution is difficult, ask what a useful improvement would look like.

#### Help: offer perspective without taking over

Once you understand the situation, ask what would be useful:

> Would you like questions, an experience from me, or my direct view?

When sharing an experience, explain the situation, what you did, and what happened. Acknowledge that their situation may be different. The mentee considers what applies and decides what to do.

Invite their own reflection before offering feedback:

> What do you think went well?

You can then ask what they would do differently. If you notice a possible blind spot, describe what you heard and invite them to examine it. For example:

> You want your manager to decide, but you said every decision still needs your approval. How do you see those two things fitting together?

Be candid and specific. Check your understanding rather than labelling the person or assuming you have the whole picture.

#### Close: leave the choice with the mentee

> What do you think you will do next?

One useful next step is often more helpful than a long list. The mentee chooses the action and follows through between meetings. At the next meeting, the mentor checks in on progress.

Finish by reflecting on the conversation:

> What was most useful?

### 4. See it in practice

North Shore Supply is a fictional distributor with eighteen employees. Revenue is growing, but delivery errors are increasing. The founder still approves most customer exceptions. Their operations leader waits for approval instead of deciding. The founder is considering replacing them.

We will compare three ways this conversation could unfold.

#### Taking over

**Mentee:** My operations leader won't make decisions. I think I need to replace them.

**Mentor:** You need a stronger operations leader. I would start recruiting and restructure the team.

The mentor has chosen a solution before understanding the situation. The mentee's own role in the problem is unexplored.

#### Helping through questions

**Mentee:** My operations leader won't make decisions. I think I need to replace them.

**Mentor:** What decisions can they make without you?

**Mentee:** Most customer exceptions still come to me.

**Mentor:** What happens when they decide without checking?

**Mentee:** I sometimes overrule them. Perhaps I have taught them to wait.

**Mentor:** What do you think you will do next?

**Mentee:** Clarify what they can decide and give that a chance before deciding whether to replace them.

The questions help the mentee notice their own part in the situation and choose an action.

#### Helping through experience

After hearing the situation, the mentor asks whether a similar experience would be useful.

**Mentor:** I faced something similar. I thought my manager wasn't taking responsibility, but I discovered I kept overruling their decisions. Agreeing on what they could decide without me helped. Your situation may be different.

**Mentor:** What, if anything, fits your situation?

**Mentee:** I want to look at my own approval habits before deciding the problem is the person.

Both questions and experience can help. In each useful version, the mentee does the thinking and makes the decision.

### 5. Try it together

Harbour Field Services is a fictional maintenance company with twelve employees. Its founder is the only person who prepares quotes. Sales have slowed because the founder cannot respond quickly enough. They want growth but worry that delegating quotes will damage margins. They are considering hiring another salesperson.

Work with your matched partner. Use this fictional situation so you can practise without sharing private business information. This exercise is not your first mentoring meeting.

1. The person playing the mentee chooses what they want help thinking through.
2. The person playing the mentor listens and asks one question at a time. Use Focus, Explore, Help and Close as useful prompts.
3. If experience would help, offer a relevant example and leave the choice with the mentee. There is no need to invent expertise or rush to advice.
4. The mentee decides what to do next. Together, identify something the mentor did that helped the mentee think for themselves.

We will then discuss as a group:

- What helped the mentee think more clearly?
- When did the mentor feel the urge to solve the problem?
- What kept the decision with the mentee?
- What would you do differently next time?

There is no single correct business answer. We are practising how to have a useful conversation.

2. **Explore:** Listen and follow the mentee's words, one short question at a time. Try “What else?”, “What is the real challenge for you?”, “What part of this is yours?”, or “What do you want to happen?”
3. **Help:** “Would you like questions, an experience from me, or my direct view?” Share experience or advice only after understanding the situation, and leave the decision with the mentee.
4. **Close:** “What do you think you will do?” Then ask, “What was most useful?”

Do not hide advice inside a question. Stop asking questions when the mentee is clear.

A useful meeting creates clarity and ownership. It does not need to solve everything.

## The first mentoring meeting

**Status: Agreed**

The first meeting begins the pair's twelve-month cycle.

Each pair books this meeting before leaving training and shares its planned date with the Chair.
The planned date does not start the cycle.

The pair should:

- Confirm how they will schedule meetings.
- Reconfirm the commitment to meet for one hour in ten of the twelve months.
- Discuss the business challenge or opportunity the mentee most wants help with now.
- Agree how they will prepare for each conversation.
- Confirm confidentiality and communication preferences.
- Arrange the next meeting.

The mentee may change what they want to work on at any time. The pair does not need to report that change to the program.

The day after the booked meeting, the mentee receives this brief email:

> Did your first mentoring meeting take place as planned? If it was rescheduled, please let us know the new date.

A genuine participant response stops that confirmation's reminders. Out-of-office messages and delivery failures do not count.

The system records a clear confirmation from the mentee. It brings unclear replies to the Chair without assuming the meeting happened.
The confirmed actual date starts the twelve-month cycle and quarterly check-ins.
If the mentee says the meeting happened on a different date, record that actual date rather than another booking. A date alone is not confirmation.
If the meeting did not happen and no new date is known, leave the pair unstarted and notify the Chair.
If the meeting moves, update the planned date and stop follow-up against the previous booking.
Send the confirmation email the day after the new date, with the same follow-up timing if unanswered.
Missing confirmation does not establish that the meeting happened. No details of the mentoring conversation are requested.

## Quarterly check-ins

**Status: Agreed**

Each active pair completes a check-in at months three, six, nine, and twelve. If a relationship ends early, a final check-in replaces later scheduled check-ins.

The timing follows the pair's first meeting date. It does not follow Example Chapter's calendar year.

The check-in records meeting frequency, brief feedback on value, and whether either person wants the Mentorship Chair to contact them. Final feedback also covers business progress and interest in mentoring again.

The check-in does not record the confidential substance of mentoring conversations.

### Check-in questions

The mentee and mentor each receive the same three questions at months three, six, and nine:

1. How many times have you met since the last check-in?
2. What value, if any, have you received from participating in this mentorship?
3. Would you like the Mentorship Chair to contact you? Yes or no.

At month twelve, or when a relationship ends early, the mentee answers:

1. How many times have you met since the last check-in?
2. What progress, if any, did this mentorship help you make in your business?
3. What value, if any, did you receive from participating?
4. Would you like the Mentorship Chair to contact you? Yes or no.

At month twelve, or when a relationship ends early, the mentor answers:

1. How many times have you met since the last check-in?
2. What value, if any, did you receive from participating as a mentor?
3. How would you feel about mentoring again?
4. Would you like the Mentorship Chair to contact you? Yes or no.

Meeting counts are numbers. Value, progress, and future-mentoring questions use free text, not rating choices. Only the contact request is yes or no. For the first check-in, count meetings since the relationship began.

Please answer briefly and do not include confidential details.

You may use the private form or reply by email with the same answers.
You do not need to enter an email answer again through the form.

AI classifies the written feedback to measure the program and identify where support may be useful. The Mentorship Chair reviews unclear answers.

Once the Chair approves a match and its actual first meeting date is confirmed, the system schedules check-in emails.

### Reminders and follow-up

The initial check-in email is day zero.

- Day seven: send a reminder if the request remains unanswered.
- Day fourteen: send a second reminder if it is still unanswered.
- Day twenty-one: notify the Chair and record `Failure: no response` if it is still unanswered.

A genuine response stops that person's no-response reminders. It does not stop reminders for the other person in the pair.
Out-of-office messages and delivery failures do not count as participant responses.

A reply does not necessarily complete a check-in. Missing answers remain outstanding and visible to the Chair.

Keep and classify the answers already provided. Missing meeting counts or contact preferences leave the check-in incomplete, without inventing answers.
An acknowledgement alone does not supply a missing outcome or clear its failure.

AI also flags feedback showing little or no value, even if the participant has not requested contact. The Chair decides whether to reach out. AI does not contact the participant about that flag automatically.

## Measuring program success

**Status: Agreed**

The check-in completion target is 100% for every required quarterly or final check-in.
Exclude quarterly requests superseded by final feedback. Previously missed deadlines remain in history.

### Classifying written feedback

AI classifies value and progress separately:

- Significant.
- Meaningful.
- Some.
- Little or none.
- Unclear.

Significant and Meaningful count as positive. The Chair reviews Unclear answers. The original response is available during the review, so the classification can be checked against what the participant actually said.

Business progress is the deciding outcome for the mentee's mentorship. A positive experience without meaningful business progress is unsuccessful. Keep value and progress separate; do not combine their scores to offset a failed progress result.

An important business decision can count as meaningful progress before financial results appear. For example: “The mentorship helped me decide to close an unprofitable product line.” Use the mentee's feedback; do not require proof or external validation. Enjoyable conversations alone do not establish business progress.

For “How would you feel about mentoring again?”, AI classifies the answer as Interested, Unsure, or Not interested, preserving any conditions mentioned during the review. Only Interested counts as positive.

The classification rules are fixed and include fictional test cases. Evaluate the configured model before live use and after changing the model or prompt. AI must not invent missing feedback or infer a participant's motivation from silence.

### Targets and calculations

The four final targets are:

- At least 80% of mentees have positive business-progress feedback.
- At least 80% of mentees have positive value feedback.
- At least 80% of mentors have positive value feedback.
- At least 80% of mentors are interested in mentoring again.

Collect feedback separately for each mentoring relationship. Count each person's role in each relationship once, including early endings.
A mentor working with two mentees contributes two separate relationship results, not one combined result.

Include that relationship-role when all its outcome answers arrive or its original twenty-one-day final-response window expires.
Outcome answers mean mentee progress and value, or mentor value and return interest.
Until then, incomplete outcome feedback remains pending across that role's measures, including answers already received.
Unclear and Unsure are received answers, not omissions. They remain non-positive under the classification rules.

`Success rate = positive classifications / all included relationship-roles of that type`

An expired nonresponse stays in the calculation and counts as failure until corrected. Unclear or Unsure is not a positive classification. A late response updates the existing result rather than adding another participant.

Partial replies do not extend the original twenty-one-day window or exclude a participant after it expires.
On day twenty-one, a missing outcome answer counts as failure only for that measure until corrected.
Preserve valid answers for the other measures. A missing meeting count or contact preference leaves completion outstanding, without replacing outcome answers.

Incomplete outcome feedback within its twenty-one-day window remains pending, not failure.
Missing meeting counts or contact preferences do not delay entry into outcome reporting.
Reports show counts, pending answers, and failures from nonresponse or missing outcome answers beside each percentage.

Quarterly nonresponse failures remain visible until corrected, but are not counted as extra people in the final calculation.

Deleting personal information preserves its contribution to overall totals under the Privacy Notice.
Keep valid outcomes. Do not count missing outcomes as failures before the original deadline.
Keep only the temporary private reporting data allowed by the Privacy Notice, then totals only. Do not retain a person-specific outcome lookup or reconnect a deleted person's results.
If withdrawal and deletion precede any final request, use the Privacy Notice's reporting-only twenty-one-day period.
Keep that contribution pending for the full period, then include valid outcomes and failures for unanswered measures.

### Cohort operating sequence

#### Before applications first open

The Mentorship Chair:

1. Confirms capacity for the cohort.
2. Confirms the maximum number of pairs.
3. Confirms the initial application opening and training options. Later cohort planning does not close applications.
4. Tests the forms and Program Status Register.
5. Confirms storage, access, retention, and deletion settings.
6. Confirms the approved AI tools, coded matching process, and feedback classification rules and tests.
7. Confirms the approved program email connection, incoming-reply processing, sending rules, and Chair notifications without unrestricted personal-inbox access.
8. Reviews participant communications.
9. Tests authorized AI actions, private backups, and recovery/export without relying on chat history.

#### As applications arrive and a cohort takes shape

The Mentorship Chair:

1. Holds a short readiness conversation with every mentee being considered.
2. Reviews mentee readiness and selects the mentees whose needs the cohort will try to serve.
3. Invites mentors and reviews public expressions of interest against those needs.
4. Holds a short conversation with any prospective mentor not already known to the Chair.
5. Requests only information needed to complete the review.
6. Removes names and contact details before AI matching.
7. Uses AI to prepare matching recommendations.
8. Reviews conflicts and match quality.
9. Approves every final match and deletes the temporary coded matching copy.
10. Confirms that every selected mentee has one suitable mentor before offering a place.

Applications stay open while this review takes place and between cohorts.
The Chair starts a cohort when enough suitable pairs are ready, without requiring ten pairs.

#### Before mentoring begins

The Mentorship Chair:

1. Introduces each approved pair.
2. Gives each pair a short, positive explanation of the proposed fit and confirms that both participants accept the commitments.
3. Arranges the joint cohort training with the chosen facilitator, who may be the Chair or a third party.
4. Records training completion.
5. Decides whether to offer a live make-up for any participant who missed the cohort session.
6. Lets fully trained pairs begin without waiting for another pair's make-up.
7. Ensures each pair books its first 3–6 meetings before leaving training and records the planned first meeting date. The pair manages its remaining calendar; the Chair does not collect it.
8. Arranges the brief confirmation email to the mentee for the day after the booked meeting.
9. Uses reminders seven and fourteen days after an unanswered confirmation email, with a Chair notification on day twenty-one.

#### During each cycle

The Mentorship Chair:

1. Keeps each matched pair visible until its actual first meeting date is confirmed.
2. Reviews exceptions to automatic first-meeting confirmation. Rescheduling changes only the planned date and confirmation timing.
3. Uses the confirmed actual date to schedule automatic check-ins at months three, six, nine, and twelve.
4. Reviews unclear feedback, incomplete replies, and processing failures. Clear form and email answers update records automatically.
5. Uses reminders on days seven and fourteen and a day-twenty-one notification and nonresponse failure for each unanswered check-in.
6. Reviews low-value flags and decides whether to contact the participant. Contacts participants who request help.
7. Checks that substantive late answers correct the same record and measures. Acknowledgements do not supply missing results.
8. If a relationship ends early, stops its scheduled check-ins and outstanding reminders, then requests final feedback with the same follow-up timing.
9. Protects participant information and completes deletion requests.

#### At the end of each cycle

The Mentorship Chair:

1. Records and classifies the mentee's final progress and value feedback.
2. Records and classifies the mentor's final value and interest in mentoring again.
3. Thanks both participants.
4. Updates aggregate measures, including early endings and final nonresponse failures after day twenty-one.
5. After the final review, deletes detailed applications and free-text responses, retaining only the approved minimum record. Late feedback can still correct that result and follows the same deletion rule.
6. Records program lessons without confidential details.
